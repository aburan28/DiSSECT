# The supervised distinguisher, defined

The outlier analysis asks whether any *single* standard curve sits far from its
simulated pool. That is a weak question. A selection bias could easily leave
every standard curve comfortably inside the bulk of the distribution while still
shifting all of them the same way on some combination of traits that no single
trait exposes.

A supervised distinguisher asks the stronger question directly: **is there any
function of the traits that separates standard curves from simulated ones?** If
the standard curves were drawn from the same process as the pool, no such
function exists beyond chance. If one exists, it is either a real difference in
how the curves were chosen or an artefact of how the pool was built, and
distinguishing those two is most of the work.

This is `dissect-ml_distinguisher`. What follows is the method, the null it is
measured against, and the conditions under which a positive result would mean
anything.

## 1. The hypothesis

Let *S* be the standard curves of one family at one bitlength and *P* the
simulated pool generated by that family's own procedure. The null hypothesis is
**exchangeability**: every curve in *S* ∪ *P* is an independent draw from one
distribution, and the standard label carries no information.

Under that null, a classifier trained to predict the label can do no better than
chance on curves it has not seen. The test statistic is the leave-one-out area
under the ROC curve (AUC), and the question is whether the observed AUC is
larger than the null produces.

## 2. The features

Feature vectors come from the same path as the outlier analysis: every trait
result for the curve, flattened, min-max scaled to [0, 1], with the coverage
filter applied. Two rules from that pipeline carry over and matter here more
than they do there.

**Filters run before scaling.** `scale_feature` scales over whatever rows it is
handed, so a row dropped afterwards has still set the feature's range. This
defect appeared three separate times in this work. Every filter enters
`build_features` as `standard_curves` or `restrict_to`, before the trait loop.

**Coverage is filtered on both sides.** A trait computed for the standard curves
but for none of the simulated ones is imputed to −1.0, outside the scaled range,
which hands the classifier a perfect separator made of nothing. Features missing
on either side are dropped before training.

## 3. The scoring protocol

Each standard curve must be scored by a model that never saw it:

- **Positives** are scored strictly leave-one-out. For each standard curve, a
  model is trained on every other curve, including the other standard curves,
  and asked for P(standard) on the held-out one.
- **Negatives** are scored in 5 folds, with all positives in training.

Pooling across bitlengths is done on the intersection of the usable features, so
every curve in one run is described by the same vector.

Two models are run. Logistic regression asks whether a *linear* combination of
traits separates; gradient-boosted trees, at depth 2, ask whether a shallow
interaction does. Agreement between them is worth more than either alone.

## 4. The null, which is the whole point

With four to six standard curves per family, the AUC by itself is
uninterpretable. A perfect-looking score on six positives is unremarkable if six
*arbitrary* curves score the same way.

So the identical procedure runs on a relabelled null. The true standard curves
are removed; a random subset of simulated curves of the same size is labelled
"standard"; the same models, the same leave-one-out protocol and the same AUC
are computed. Repeating this 20 to 30 times gives the distribution of AUC values
the method produces **when there is definitionally nothing to find.** The
reported p-value is the fraction of null draws reaching the observed AUC.

This is what makes the output mean anything. It calibrates against the
small-sample behaviour of the estimator instead of against an asymptotic
approximation that six points do not earn.

## 5. Features that must be excluded, and why

A distinguisher finds the easiest separator, not the most meaningful one. On its
first run this one found two, and both were properties of the pool rather than
of the curves. They are excluded by default, via `--drop-prefix` and
`--drop-suffix`.

**The simulator keeps the smaller root of b.** X9.62 fixes *b* only up to sign:
*b*²·*r* ≡ *a*³ (mod *p*) has two roots, *b* and *p* − *b*. Every simulated X9.62
curve takes the smaller, so all of them have *b* < *p*/2. The standards took
whichever the derivation produced: `secp128r1` sits at 0.908·*p* and `secp224r1`
at 0.703·*p*. After scaling, those two land at exactly 1.0 and a single tree
split separates them perfectly. Because every pool prime is 3 mod 4, the two
roots are quadratic twists rather than isomorphic curves, so this is a genuine
gap in the simulated space — the pool never contains a curve whose *larger* root
passed the order test — but it is a fact about the generator, and the standards
fall on either side as a coin flip would.

**Brainpool `t1` is `r1`.** The two share every trait value. Leaving one out
while its twin remains in training lets the model memorise rather than
generalise, which is enough on its own to produce AUC 0.96.

Both are instances of one rule: **exclude any feature that encodes a generation
convention rather than a property of the curve.** The
[parameter-form survey]({{ '/parameter_forms.html' | relative_url }}) exists to
enumerate candidates — generator coordinate size and seed length are two more
that would leak if a trait ever read them.

## 6. Results

With both exclusions applied:

| family | model | n | leave-one-out AUC | null AUC | P(null ≥ observed) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SECG vs x962_sim | logistic | 6 | 0.52 | 0.45 ± 0.20 | 0.37 |
| SECG vs x962_sim | gradient boosting | 6 | 0.45 | 0.44 ± 0.15 | 0.45 |
| Brainpool | logistic | 4 | 0.62 | 0.42 ± 0.20 | 0.17 |
| Brainpool | gradient boosting | 4 | 0.34 | 0.30 ± 0.18 | 0.40 |

Nothing separates. Before the exclusions, gradient boosting on SECG reached
0.69 and Brainpool logistic regression reached 0.96; both figures were the two
artefacts above and neither survived them.

## 7. How to read a result

The null's standard deviation of 0.15 to 0.20 sets the bar. **An AUC below
roughly 0.85 is not evidence of anything** at these sample sizes, and this is a
property of having four to six standard curves, not of the models. Adding traits
does not fix it; only more standard curves would, and there are no more.

A result above that bar is a starting point, not a conclusion. The question to
ask first is the one that killed both first-run positives:

> Is the feature driving this a property of the curve, or of the generator?

Check which features carry the model, take the top few, and compare the
standard curves against the pool on each one directly. If the separation comes
from a convention — a canonical root, a coordinate size, a duplicated curve —
the finding is about the simulator. Only when it survives that does it become a
statement about how the curves were chosen.

## 8. What the method cannot do

- **It is blind to a weakness no trait names.** The distinguisher searches the
  span of the trait set. A curve selected for a property nobody has thought to
  measure passes every trait by construction, which is precisely the scenario
  behind the seed-provenance concern.
- **It needs a comparable pool.** `secp256k1`, the Koblitz curves and every
  binary-field curve have none in the database and cannot be tested at all.
- **A negative result is bounded by the power above**, and should be read as *no
  evidence of a difference*, never as evidence of no difference.

## Reproducing

```shell
dissect-ml_distinguisher --category secg --sim-category x962_sim \
    --bits 128 160 192 224 256

# the uncorrected run, reproducing both artefacts
dissect-ml_distinguisher --category brainpool --sim-category brainpool_sim \
    --bits 160 192 224 256 --drop-prefix --drop-suffix
```
